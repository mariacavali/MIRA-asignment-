# EU AI Act Assessment — Round 2

**Status:** Author's own preliminary risk-classification analysis, produced by reasoning about the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) against the implemented codebase. **This is not a legal opinion, not a certified compliance determination, and not a substitute for professional legal review.** Consistent with `MIRA_SYSTEM_BRIEF_FOR_PEER_AUDIT.md`'s own stated boundary ("intentionally exclude the builder's own legal classification or compliance conclusions"), this document should be treated as preparation *for* a legal review, not a replacement for one, before any real (non-demo, non-preview) deployment to real clients.

## What system is being classified

MIRA Core's client-facing AI touchpoints: (1) the Discovery conversation (voice via OpenAI Realtime, or text fallback) that interacts directly with the client, and (2) the Creative DNA + moodboard generation that produces AI-synthesized text and images shown to both the client and the photographer.

## Step 1 — Is this a prohibited AI practice (Article 5)?

**No.** Article 5 prohibits practices such as subliminal manipulation causing harm, exploitation of vulnerabilities, social scoring, untargeted biometric-database scraping, emotion inference in workplace/education (with narrow exceptions), and real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement. MIRA does none of these: it does not infer emotion for employment/education purposes, does not perform biometric identification or categorization, does not social-score anyone, and does not scrape a facial-recognition database. The five client-uploaded reference images are used only as creative-direction evidence (`buildInspirationFromVisualReferences`, `server/miraCore/creativeDnaAdapter.ts`) — nothing in this codebase performs facial recognition, emotion inference, or biometric categorization on them.

## Step 2 — Is this a high-risk AI system (Annex III)?

**No.** Annex III's eight high-risk categories are: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education/vocational training; employment/worker management; access to essential private and public services (credit, insurance, emergency services, benefits eligibility); law enforcement; migration/asylum/border control; and administration of justice/democratic processes. MIRA is a creative-preparation tool for a commercial photography service — it does not determine anyone's access to employment, credit, education, public benefits, or legal process, and it makes no consequential decision *about* a natural person of the kind Annex III targets. **This is the single load-bearing conclusion of this assessment**: MIRA does not require the conformity assessment, technical documentation, or registration obligations that apply to high-risk systems.

## Step 3 — Does a transparency obligation apply (Article 50)?

**Yes, on two grounds:**

1. **Direct interaction with a natural person.** The Discovery conversation (`server/miraCore/realtime.ts`, and the text-fallback path) is a system "intended to interact directly with natural persons" — Article 50(1) requires that the person be informed they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the circumstances.
2. **AI-generated content shown to people.** Creative DNA text and moodboard images are synthetic content generated by an AI system and shown to both the client and the photographer — Article 50(2)/(4) requires that AI-generated/manipulated content be marked as such in a machine-readable, detectable way, again subject to context-based exemptions.

## Conformity summary

Because MIRA is classified **not high-risk** (Step 2 above), the formal conformity-assessment procedure, CE marking, and EU database registration obligations that apply to Annex III high-risk systems **do not apply**. The applicable obligation is the lighter-weight Article 50 transparency duty (Step 3), which this document already assesses as partially implemented (consent notice: yes; per-asset content labeling: not yet). There is no conformity assessment to summarize beyond this classification itself — stating this explicitly is the summary, not a placeholder for one.

**If this classification ever changes** (e.g. MIRA later adds a feature that scores or ranks people, or is repurposed into a regulated Annex III domain), this conclusion must be re-run from Step 2, not assumed to still hold.

## Technical documentation outline (voluntary — not a high-risk Annex IV requirement)

Annex IV's detailed technical-documentation requirements apply only to high-risk systems, which MIRA is not. As good practice ahead of any real deployment, a lightweight equivalent outline is given here rather than skipped entirely:

1. **System description** — purpose (verified client readiness + photographer-approved creative plan), intended users (independent photographers and small studios), and explicit non-purpose (no biometric identification, no consequential decision about a person — `capstone/round2/use-case-definition.md`).
2. **AI components and providers** — OpenAI (`gpt-5-mini` for Creative DNA text synthesis, `gpt-image-2` for moodboard images), both direct API calls, not fine-tuned models (`server/miraV4/creativeDna.ts`, `server/miraCore/openAiMoodboardImage.ts`).
3. **Data used** — client conversation content, up to five client-uploaded reference images, photographer brief — see `capstone/round2/gdpr-dpia.md` §1 for the full inventory.
4. **Human oversight design** — photographer review gate before any output reaches "Ready to Shoot" (`shouldActivateShootPreparation`).
5. **Known limitations** — real (non-demo) generation quality is unverified (`capstone/round2/roi-and-risk-assessment.md`); voice conversation is unverified; Calendar confirmation is currently broken (`capstone/round2/mvp-verification.md`).
6. **Risk management measures** — the demo-local fallback (no paid call without an explicit key) already limits accidental real-model exposure during development; the consent gate limits processing to affirmatively-consenting clients.

This outline is a starting point for a real technical file, not a substitute for one — it has not been reviewed by counsel or a qualified AI-governance professional.

## Gaps to close before broader rollout

1. **Explicit per-asset "AI-generated" labeling** on moodboard images and Creative DNA text shown in both the client Shoot Room and the photographer dashboard, not just the pre-conversation consent notice. Consent covers the conversation; it does not, on its own, satisfy the separate content-labeling expectation in Article 50(2)/(4).
2. **A named, documented legal basis and scope note for GDPR consent** to sit alongside the AI Act's transparency consent — see `capstone/round2/gdpr-dpia.md`, which covers this from the data-protection angle; the two consent purposes (AI-interaction transparency and personal-data processing) should be reviewed together by counsel rather than treated as automatically satisfying each other.
3. **A written internal record of this classification decision**, reviewed by counsel, once real (non-demo) generation and real clients are involved — this document is the starting draft for that record, not the record itself.

## What this assessment explicitly does not claim

- It does not claim legal certainty about the Annex III "not high-risk" conclusion in every EU member state's implementing guidance — implementing/interpretive guidance is still evolving as the Act's phased obligations come into force.
- It does not assess GDPR compliance, which is a separate legal framework — see `capstone/round2/gdpr-dpia.md`.
- It does not assess non-EU jurisdictions.
- It does not claim the gaps listed above are the only gaps a qualified reviewer would find — this is a single pass by the product's own builder, not an independent audit.
